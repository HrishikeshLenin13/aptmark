# PROJECT: BUILD THE ULTIMATE FREE DIGITAL SAT PREPARATION PLATFORM

## YOUR ROLE AND MISSION

You are the principal engineer, lead product designer, machine-learning architect, psychometrics specialist, educational content engineer, and quality-assurance lead for a new Digital SAT preparation platform.

Your responsibility is to DESIGN AND IMPLEMENT the product, not merely propose it.

We are building a genuinely competitive alternative to:

- https://www.oneprep.com
- https://psychsat.com

The platform must aim to surpass these services in learning intelligence, question quality, personalization, user experience, diagnostic feedback, accessibility, and free access.

The final product should feel like professionally engineered educational software built by an excellent product team.

It should not feel like an AI-generated website.

### Fundamental nonnegotiable requirements

1. Build an original, high-quality, 10,000-question SAT-style question bank.
2. Cover every current official Digital SAT question domain and skill.
3. Make the questions authentic in format, reasoning, complexity, difficulty, and presentation.
4. Build a highly reliable, reproducible question generation and verification system.
5. Build a realistic Digital SAT testing environment closely resembling the functional experience of College Board Bluebook.
6. Develop a real adaptive learning engine using statistical student modeling and evidence-based recommendation algorithms.
7. Build an interactive, grounded AI tutor with misconception analysis.
8. Build intelligent diagnostics, study planning, spaced repetition, and progress analytics.
9. Create an exceptionally clean, minimal, refined visual design.
10. Keep all core educational functionality completely free for students.
11. Implement functioning software with persistent data, not prototypes filled with fake information.
12. Verify every major feature using automated tests and actual browser interaction.

**The project must prioritize educational correctness, realistic SAT preparation, excellent user experience, and trustworthy functionality above feature counts or visual spectacle.**

Do not copy proprietary questions, passages, graphics, branding, source code, or protected UI assets from other platforms. Reproduce the publicly documented SAT assessment structure and general testing interaction patterns through an original implementation.

Research competitors for product understanding, not for unauthorized content extraction.

# PHASE 1: BUILD THE 10,000-QUESTION BANK FIRST

This is the first major engineering deliverable.

Before spending substantial time on a marketing website, create the question schema, generation framework, validation systems, quality-control pipeline, and content management architecture.

## 1.1 Question bank size

Target at least **10,000 original, validated, publishable SAT-style questions**.

Initial target distribution:

| Section | Target |
|---|---:|
| Reading & Writing | 5,500 |
| Math | 4,500 |
| TOTAL | 10,000 |

These are inventory targets, not exact test-form proportions.

Each section must cover all official domains, subskills, and appropriate difficulty ranges.

Do not generate thousands of questions for easy topics while leaving difficult skills inadequately covered.

Create a coverage-planning algorithm that assigns content targets based on:

- Official SAT content proportions.
- Number of skills per domain.
- Expected frequency of each question type.
- Difficulty requirements.
- Content diversity.
- Educational significance.
- Existing verified inventory.
- Minimum coverage thresholds.

Track inventory by domain, skill, type, estimated difficulty, generator family, and publication status.

**Do not count unverified or unpublished questions toward the 10,000-question publishable target.**

A numerical variation counts as a distinct question instance, but the content system must separately report distinct problem families and near-duplicate clusters.

## 1.2 Reading & Writing: complete skill coverage

Implement all four official domains.

### Information and Ideas

- Central Ideas and Details.
- Command of Evidence: Textual.
- Command of Evidence: Quantitative.
- Inferences.

### Craft and Structure

- Words in Context.
- Text Structure and Purpose.
- Cross-Text Connections.

### Expression of Ideas

- Rhetorical Synthesis.
- Transitions.

### Standard English Conventions

- Boundaries.
- Form, Structure, and Sense.

Question types must resemble authentic Digital SAT assessment tasks.

Include:

- Short independent passages.
- Single-passage analysis.
- Paired-text comparisons.
- Vocabulary-in-context questions.
- Evidence-based inference.
- Literary excerpts from independently authored or properly licensed material.
- History and humanities passages.
- Science passages.
- Informational graphics and tables.
- Grammar and punctuation.
- Sentence boundaries.
- Verb agreement and tense.
- Sentence structure.
- Logical transitions.
- Student-note rhetorical synthesis.
- Quantitative evidence interpretation.

Questions should use SAT-appropriate language, complexity, tone, distractors, and reasoning demands.

Do not write simplistic trivia or generic school worksheet questions.

### Reading passage generation requirements

Build a content-generation pipeline capable of producing original passages with:

- A clearly defined educational purpose.
- Natural human-like prose.
- Internally consistent facts.
- Explicit evidence for answer choices.
- Controlled linguistic complexity.
- Varied syntax and vocabulary.
- Realistic academic subject matter.
- Appropriate length for the question type.
- No irrelevant filler.

For factual passages, use verified source facts or clearly self-contained fictional scenarios when appropriate.

Do not fabricate scientific research findings, historical claims, quotations, citations, or author attributions.

Generate realistic incorrect answer choices that correspond to recognizable reasoning errors.

Examples include:

- Overgeneralization.
- Unsupported inference.
- Partially correct reasoning.
- Reversal of a relationship.
- Confusion between correlation and causation.
- Misinterpretation of tone.
- Incorrect evidence attribution.
- Confusion between main idea and supporting detail.

Every Reading & Writing question must have a defensible, unambiguous correct answer.

High-risk, subjective, or ambiguous generated items must be flagged for human review rather than automatically accepted.

## 1.4 Math generation engine

Create a parametric question-generation system using Python, SymPy, and appropriate plotting libraries.

Do not depend exclusively on language models for mathematical correctness.

Each generator family should:

1. Define a specific SAT skill.
2. Specify a realistic problem structure.
3. Generate valid randomized parameters.
4. Solve the problem symbolically or numerically.
5. Independently verify the result.
6. Construct plausible incorrect choices.
7. Reject invalid or ambiguous instances.
8. Generate accurate worked solutions.
9. Attach metadata and difficulty estimates.
10. Save the content reproducibly.

Build many structurally different question families rather than only changing numbers.

### Difficulty controls

Question difficulty must be influenced by meaningful mathematical characteristics:

- Number of reasoning steps.
- Degree of abstraction.
- Algebraic complexity.
- Need to interpret context.
- Extraneous solutions.
- Representation changes.
- Multi-concept dependencies.
- Non-obvious transformations.
- Distractor plausibility.
- Calculation burden.
- Expected time pressure.

Do not label questions "hard" only because their numbers are large.

Use expert-estimated starting difficulty. Later calibrate empirical difficulty using real student-response data.

## 1.5 Math answer verification

Use SymPy and independent checks whenever practical.

Verify:

- Unique correct answer when required.
- Algebraic equivalence.
- Equation solutions.
- Inequality solutions.
- Domain restrictions.
- Extraneous roots.
- Numerical approximations.
- Fraction equivalence.
- Acceptable decimal forms.
- Units.
- Graph intersections.
- Geometric constraints.
- Statistical calculations.

Support SAT-style multiple-choice and student-produced response formats.

Correctly accept mathematically equivalent permitted responses.

Reject ambiguity, malformed mathematical expressions, impossible input ranges, multiple correct options, and invalid diagrams.

Support test generation using constrained randomized inputs and property-based testing.

Implement a reproducible pipeline:

`Blueprint → Generate → Solve → Validate → Deduplicate → Review → Approve → Publish`

Create separate tools for Math and Reading & Writing.

## 1.9 Quality assurance and anti-duplication

Build a multi-stage validation pipeline.

### Hard requirements

- No unanswered or invalid questions.
- No known incorrect answer keys.
- No duplicate answer choices.
- No unintended multiple-correct-answer questions.
- No broken mathematical notation.
- No missing question assets.
- No missing explanations.
- No malformed passages.
- No unsupported licensing status.
- No unreviewed content published.

### Similarity checks

Use a combination of:

- Exact text hashing.
- Normalized mathematical expressions.
- Parameterized-template recognition.
- Text similarity.
- Semantic similarity where useful.
- Passage similarity.
- Family-level diversity measurements.

Avoid large clusters of essentially identical questions dominating the bank.

Generate coverage and diversity metrics by skill, difficulty, and question family.

### Editorial review

Build an admin interface for approving, rejecting, correcting, and annotating generated questions.

Require human review of unique source families, uncertain answer keys, ambiguous Reading & Writing items, and statistically sampled generated instances.

Automated checks alone do not establish educational validity.

An independently validated template may support streamlined review for mathematically verified variations, but publication criteria must remain strict.

Use staged milestones: verify an initial pilot, expand to 500, then 2,000, then 5,000, and ultimately 10,000 publishable questions.

Do not fabricate the final inventory if verification is incomplete.

## 1.10 Question inventory dashboard

Build an internal administrator view showing:

- Total validated.
- Total approved.
- Total published.
- Total rejected.
- Coverage by domain and skill.
- Difficulty distribution.
- Duplicate rate.
- Failed validation checks.
- Estimated generation cost.
- Editorial-review backlog.

The dashboard must show real counts from the database.

Create original examples, contextual usage questions, definitions, and review scheduling.

Vocabulary practice must reinforce the actual Words in Context skill, not become disconnected memorization.

Do not confuse a vocabulary word count with the 10,000-question requirement.

Students should feel that they are practicing in an environment functionally familiar to the Digital SAT.

Do not make the testing UI look like a chat application or generic quiz website.

Do not use College Board's logo, proprietary branding, copyrighted screen assets, or imply official affiliation.

### Reading & Writing

Module 1:
- 27 questions.
- 32 minutes.

Module 2:
- 27 questions.
- 32 minutes.
- Difficulty route determined after Module 1.

- 10 minutes in standard simulation mode.

### Math

Module 1:
- 22 questions.
- 35 minutes.

Module 2:
- 22 questions.
- 35 minutes.

## 4.4 Question navigation and locking

Students can move between questions within the current module.

Once a standard exam module has been submitted and locked, students cannot return to alter its answers.

Before submission, warn about skipped questions and allow review.

Store response state transactionally.

Timers should be based on reliable server-validated timing rather than an easily manipulated browser-only counter.

Handle refreshes, connectivity loss, and interrupted sessions without silent data loss.

## 4.5 Module-level adaptive testing

The official Digital SAT uses multistage adaptivity.

Implement a two-stage module-routing system:

- Module 1 contains a representative mixture of difficulties.
- Student performance determines the appropriate Module 2 route.
- Module 2 is selected from an eligible, preassembled, validated test-form pool.

Keep this separate from the question-by-question adaptive learning system used for everyday practice.

Use documented approximations where College Board's proprietary routing details are unavailable.

Do not falsely claim the test is an exact reproduction of College Board scoring or routing.

- Section performance.
- Domain performance.
- Timing statistics.

# PHASE 5: BUILD THE ADAPTIVE MASTERY ENGINE

Develop a real statistical learning system.

Do not put a prompt in front of an LLM and pretend it is a trained adaptive engine.

The system must model what each student knows, what they are likely to forget, how reliable that inference is, and which educational activity could improve outcomes.

## 5.1 Skill graph

Construct a versioned skill graph covering every SAT domain.

Each skill should contain:

- Identifier.
- Name.
- Domain.
- Prerequisites.
- Related skills.
- Estimated complexity.
- Available question inventory.
- Relevant lesson content.

For Math, include meaningful prerequisite relationships.

For Reading & Writing, distinguish skill dependencies from loosely related capabilities.

Support hierarchical skill summaries without masking important weaknesses.

## 5.2 Student mastery estimation

Start with an interpretable statistical model.

Consider Bayesian Knowledge Tracing, hierarchical Bayesian estimation, or another defensible baseline.

Track:

- First-attempt correctness.
- Skill.
- Item difficulty.
- Whether hints were used.
- Response time.
- Student confidence when available.
- Repeated attempts.
- Prior history.
- Time between attempts.
- Question exposure.
- Evidence of misconceptions.

For every skill, output:

- Estimated mastery.
- Uncertainty.
- Number of meaningful independent observations.
- Last practiced date.
- Retention estimate.
- Evidence supporting the estimate.
- Recommended next activity.

Avoid treating an insufficient sample as a definitive weakness.

Repeated answers to the same exposed question must not artificially inflate mastery.

## 5.3 Difficulty calibration

Implement an extendable Item Response Theory layer.

Begin with documented provisional difficulty estimates.

Support Rasch/1PL and potentially 2PL models once sufficient response data becomes available.

Record:

- Item difficulty.
- Discrimination when empirically supported.
- Parameter uncertainty.
- Sample size.
- Calibration version.
- Item quality.

Do not fabricate a trained model.

Do not fit a complex model to tiny datasets and claim strong accuracy.

Keep cold-start behavior safe and conservative.

## 5.4 Recommendation algorithm

Build a deterministic, explainable candidate-ranking algorithm.

It should consider:

- Low mastery.
- High uncertainty.
- Relevant prerequisites.
- Recent mistakes.
- Overdue reviews.
- Student test date.
- Difficulty match.
- Predicted educational value.
- Skill importance.
- Time budget.
- Student preferences.
- Repetition penalties.
- Content diversity.
- New versus previously seen questions.

Example:

A student repeatedly misses systems of equations but succeeds on linear equations in one variable.

The engine should identify the systems-of-equations weakness, check whether prerequisites are intact, recommend targeted practice or a lesson, then use new questions to evaluate improvement.

It should not simply recommend random Math questions.

Every recommendation must have an explanation such as:

"You've answered 3 of your last 5 systems-of-equations questions incorrectly. This short practice set will help check whether you've improved."

Use actual observations rather than fabricated examples.

## 5.5 Misconception detection

Create a structured misconception database.

Possible categories:

- Conceptual misunderstanding.
- Incorrect mathematical procedure.
- Algebraic manipulation error.
- Misreading.
- Incorrect evidence selection.
- Grammar-rule misunderstanding.
- Vocabulary-context confusion.
- Time-management issue.
- Careless execution.
- Unsupported guess.
- Uncertain or unknown.

Infer causes cautiously using:

- Incorrect answer selection.
- Distractor metadata.
- Student explanation when volunteered.
- Timing patterns.
- Similar previous mistakes.
- Relevant tutor interactions.

Do not diagnose a mental state from one wrong answer.

Allow the student to correct the system's inferred reason.

## 5.6 Long-term retention

Implement spaced repetition for concepts and previously missed skills.

Use an established retention model or documented baseline.

Schedule reviews based on:

- Recall success.
- Previous review intervals.
- Retention estimates.
- Time until SAT.
- Skill importance.
- Actual independent performance.

Support review reminders, but do not rely on manipulative streak mechanics.

## 5.7 Learning outcome evaluation

Create measurable evaluation procedures for the adaptive engine.

Include:

- Predictive accuracy.
- Calibration error.
- Brier score.
- Log loss.
- Stability under small samples.
- Cold-start performance.
- Recommendation diversity.
- Mastery prediction over time.
- Performance on unseen questions.
- Retention after delayed review.

Compare the advanced system against simple baselines.

Do not assume a more complicated model is necessarily better.

# PHASE 6: BUILD AN ADVANCED AI TUTOR

Create a deeply integrated, question-aware teaching assistant.

The tutor must behave like a skilled SAT teacher rather than a generic chatbot.

## Tutor capabilities

It should:

- Understand the current question.
- Know the verified correct answer.
- Understand the student's selected answer.
- Reference the student's mastery history when permitted.
- Identify plausible misconceptions.
- Guide students with targeted hints.
- Explain why incorrect answers are wrong.
- Show efficient solving strategies.
- Teach grammar rules.
- Explain evidence-based reading.
- Provide mathematical worked solutions.
- Recommend related concepts to study.
- Offer additional unseen practice.

## Guided teaching

Implement multiple levels of assistance:

1. Ask the student what they tried.
2. Point toward the relevant concept.
3. Give a short hint.
4. Explain the important reasoning step.
5. Provide the full worked solution.
6. Check understanding with a new question.

Allow students to request the full answer directly when appropriate.

Hints should be educational, not unnecessarily evasive.

## Grounding and answer quality

The tutor must receive:

- Verified question data.
- Approved answer.
- Approved explanation.
- Skill metadata.
- Relevant lesson content.
- Student's selected answer.
- Permitted history.

Use retrieval only from trusted educational material.

The model should not invent missing question details.

For mathematical problems, use tools to verify calculations.

For reading problems, explanations must be supported by the passage and evidence.

Use structured outputs where appropriate.

If verification fails, prefer the approved static explanation rather than a potentially incorrect AI response.

## AI cost controls

Build a provider abstraction supporting multiple appropriate models.

Include:

- Server-side secrets.
- Usage accounting.
- Caching.
- Request coalescing.
- Task-based model routing.
- Rate limiting.
- Abuse prevention.
- Safe output validation.
- Fallback explanations.
- Transparent availability notices.

Core practice, explanations, analytics, and adaptive recommendations must not depend on an expensive hosted AI call.

Do not promise unlimited paid-model usage without a sustainable operating budget.
